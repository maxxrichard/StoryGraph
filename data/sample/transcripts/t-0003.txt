I came to the steppe in the first week of May, when the ground was still frozen a hand's width down and the sky was the colour of skimmed milk. The herders I was to live with for a year met me at the end of the road, which is to say at the point where the truck could no longer continue, and we rode the rest of the way. I had not been on a horse since I was nine. Nobody mentioned this, which I understood later was a kindness.
The first month I learned the words for weather. There are, in the dialect of that valley, fourteen distinct words for wind, and I was given to understand that this was considered a somewhat impoverished vocabulary compared with the valley to the north. I learned to tie the felt. I learned to milk a mare, badly, and then adequately. I learned that the dogs would not bite me once I stopped flinching, and that this was a general principle.
In July the family moved the herd to the summer pasture, a two-day journey across a river that had been a trickle in May and was now a brown roar. We crossed at dusk. Halfway across the youngest daughter's horse stumbled and for a long moment I watched her go under and come up and go under again, and her father did not move, and then she was up, laughing, on the far bank, and he still did not move, and I understood that I had seen something about fear and about trust that I would be a long time unpacking.
By September the grass was gold and the light came in low and long and the children had taught me enough of their songs that I could join in without embarrassing anyone. I wrote in my notebook: I have stopped counting days. I think this is what I came for. I think I did not know that this is what I came for.
