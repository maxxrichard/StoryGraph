Chapter one. The keeper's log began the way every log begins, with the weather. Fog, thick as wool, rolling in off the sound before dawn. Mara had kept the light at Varden Point for eleven years and she had never once missed an entry. Tonight she wrote the date and then stopped, pen hovering, because the boat was back. She could hear it before she saw it, the slap of oars that were not quite in time, as if the rower had learned on a different sea.
Her brother had told her, years ago, that the lighthouse would make her strange. He had meant it kindly. He had not been wrong. She pulled on the oilskin and took the lamp and went down the hundred and twelve steps to the landing, and the boat was there, and it was empty, and the oars were shipped neatly as if someone had just that moment stepped ashore.
Chapter two. In the morning the harbourmaster came out with the supply tender and she told him about the boat. He looked at it for a long time. It's the Ansel boat, he said finally. It went down off the Teeth in 'ninety-one with all hands. He did not say anything else, and she did not ask, and they unloaded the paraffin in silence. After he left she sat in the lamp room and watched the fog come apart in long tatters and thought about her brother, and about all the things the sea gives back.
The weeks after were quiet in the way that things are quiet before a storm. She found herself listening at night. The gulls stopped coming to the landing. On the ninth night there were footsteps on the stairs, slow and wet, and she lay very still until morning and told herself it was the wind, and in the log she wrote only: fog, light steady, no vessels sighted.
Chapter three. The truth, when it came, came in daylight, which somehow made it worse. A letter, forwarded from the mainland, from a solicitor in the city. Her brother was dead. He had died at sea, eleven years ago, in the week she first climbed the hundred and twelve steps. Everything since, every letter in his hand, every kindly warning, every postcard from a port she had never heard of, had come from somewhere she did not have a word for. She read the letter twice. Then she went down to the landing and the boat was gone, and the oars with it, and the water was flat and grey and gave nothing back at all.
